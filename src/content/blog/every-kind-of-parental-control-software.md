Most parents reach this decision through a brand name. Someone at the school gate
mentioned Bark, or a search put Qustodio at the top of the page, and the research
starts from there.

The brands are mostly not competing with each other. They are answers to four or
five different questions, and it is much easier to choose once you know which
question you are actually asking. So here is the whole landscape, including the
parts we are not in and the parts that cost nothing.

## The controls already on the phone

Both platforms ship parental controls at no extra cost, and plenty of families
never turn them on.

[Google Family Link](https://families.google/familylink/) covers Android and
Chromebooks: daily screen limits, per-app limits, approval before an app
installs, SafeSearch, location on a map, and a remote lock. Google states it is
built for children under 13, or the applicable age in your country, and that
children over that age can manage their own account.

[Apple's Screen Time](https://support.apple.com/en-us/105121) is built into
iPhone, iPad and Mac. Apple's own support
documentation lists purchase and download approval, hiding built-in apps,
content ratings for music and video, web filtering with an approved-sites mode,
restrictions on Game Center messaging, and locked privacy settings.

Neither one reaches across ecosystems, which is the practical catch. If the
household runs an Android phone, an iPad and a school Chromebook, no single
built-in tool covers all three.

For a lot of families with children under about eleven, the built-in controls are
genuinely enough. They are worth exhausting before you pay for anything.

## Monitoring apps

[Bark](https://www.bark.us/) is the best known. These tools watch what moves through the phone —
messages, social accounts, email — and alert a parent when something matches a
concern, rather than handing over a full transcript. That restraint is a
deliberate design decision and the main thing that separates monitoring from
surveillance.

The category suits older children who are already on social platforms, where the
risk is contact you cannot preview and content you would never think to block.
It is a poor fit for a seven-year-old with a tablet, who has nothing to monitor
yet.

## Filters and time-limit apps

[Qustodio](https://www.qustodio.com/en/), [Canopy](https://canopy.us/) and
[Aura](https://www.aura.com/) sit here, along with a long tail of smaller apps.
The feature set is the classic one: blocked categories, schedules, per-app
limits, usually location. Qustodio runs a free tier alongside its paid plans.

The reason to pay for one of these is usually coverage rather than capability.
They work across mixed-device households where Family Link and Screen Time each
only see half the picture.

## Phones sold already restricted

[Gabb](https://gabb.com/plans/), [Pinwheel](https://www.pinwheel.com/) and
[Troomi](https://troomi.com/) sell hardware with the restrictions built in. There
is no app store to police because there is no app store. Gabb lists a device at
$169.99 on its plans page, with a monthly service plan on top; the others follow
the same shape of buy the phone, then pay monthly.

This is the strongest option for a genuine first phone, and the most expensive
one, because you are buying a device the family did not previously need. It also
has a shelf life. The restrictions that make it right at nine are the
restrictions a thirteen-year-old will campaign against, and the usual exit is
another phone purchase.

## Launchers

A launcher replaces the home screen on a phone the family already owns. The child
sees a controlled set of apps; the device underneath stays an ordinary phone, so
the same hardware can relax as the child gets older without anybody buying
anything.

Suvi is in this category, so treat what we say about it accordingly. We built it
because the second-device cost and the upgrade cliff both seemed avoidable. If
Family Link already covers your household, though, use Family Link — it is free,
it is good, and we would rather you spent the money on something else.

## What none of these categories do

Every option above operates on the device. None of them are present in the
conversation where a child decides whether to reply to a stranger, and the
serious harms of the last few years have run through direct messages rather than
through the web pages a filter can see. We wrote about that gap separately in
[what parental controls actually block](/blog/what-parental-controls-actually-block/).

Pew Research Center surveyed 3,054 parents of children aged 12 and under in May
2025 and found that 42% say they could be doing better at managing their child's
screen time. No product in this list moves that number on its own.

## Picking one without over-buying

A reasonable order of operations:

- **Turn on what you already have**, and give it a month. Most families discover
  the built-in controls do more than they expected.
- **Name the gap before you pay.** "It does not cover the iPad" and "I cannot see
  who is messaging her" lead to completely different products.
- **Match the tool to the age.** Monitoring an eight-year-old produces nothing;
  a locked-down handset for a fifteen-year-old produces a workaround.
- **Check the price yourself.** Everything in this category reprices constantly,
  and comparison articles — this one included — go stale. The vendor's own page
  is the only number worth trusting.

If you finish this and conclude the free tools are fine, that is a real answer,
and it is the right one more often than the market would suggest.

At some point the limits stop working and it is not obvious why. The daily cap
is set, bedtime is configured, and somehow there is still YouTube at eleven at
night.

Usually it is one of four gaps rather than anything sophisticated. All four are
checkable in a few minutes, and knowing about them is more useful than
discovering them by accident six months in.

## The system clock

The most common one, and the least technical. Time limits are enforced against
the phone's clock, so a clock that has been moved back is a limit that has not
been reached yet. Set the phone to 7pm and a 9pm bedtime is two hours away
again.

The fix is to make sure automatic date and time is on and cannot be changed. In
Family Link, restrict access to date and time changes under the device settings
for that child. It takes a minute and closes the simplest gap there is.

## The accessibility menu

On some Android versions the accessibility shortcut can be used to reach recent
apps or settings from a screen that is supposed to be locked, which lets an app
Family Link has just closed be reopened.

Go to **Settings → Accessibility** on the child's phone and turn off the
accessibility menu and any shortcuts they do not genuinely need. While you are
there, look at which apps have been granted accessibility permissions and remove
any that have no business holding them — it is a powerful permission and an
unusual one for a game to want.

The obvious caution: if your child actually uses accessibility features, leave
them alone. The point is to remove what is unused, not to take away something
they need.

## Places the controls cannot see

Some manufacturers ship a private space that parental controls do not inspect.
Samsung's Secure Folder is the widely reported example: apps and files inside it
sit outside what Family Link reports on.

Check whether the phone has one and whether it is set up. This is worth
approaching as a conversation rather than a raid, because the feature has
legitimate uses and finding it is not by itself evidence of anything.

## Apps that did not come from the Play Store

Android permits installing apps from outside the Play Store, which means every
Play Store restriction you configured can be sidestepped by an app that never
went through it. Alternative stores and downloaded install files both do this,
and a VPN installed that way will route around network-level filtering as well.

Look at whether installing from unknown sources is permitted, and check the app
list against what you approved. A VPN on a ten-year-old's phone is worth asking
about.

## Where this stops working

Every fix above is worth doing, and none of them settle the matter.

Reviewers who test these products professionally treat "how easily can a child
get around it" as a standard scoring criterion, which tells you how routine this
is. The technical measures move the difficulty up a step; they do not remove the
step. A determined thirteen-year-old with time and an internet connection is a
harder adversary than a settings menu, and the gap widens every year as they get
more capable and the software does not.

The uncomfortable part is that success at this game is not obviously good. A
child who cannot get around the rules has not learned to keep them — they have
learned that they are being managed, and quite often that the interesting
problem is the lock rather than whatever the lock was protecting them from.

So close the four gaps, because leaving them open is careless. Then treat the
next bypass as information rather than a defeat. A child working hard to get
more time is telling you the current settlement does not work for them, and
that is a conversation with a solution in it. The clock fix is not.

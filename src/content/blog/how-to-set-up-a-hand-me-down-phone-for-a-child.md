The cheapest good first phone is usually one the family already owns. A
three-year-old Android that a parent has stopped using does everything a
ten-year-old needs, and it arrives without the ceremony of a new box, which is
its own quiet advantage.

The catch is that a handed-down phone carries its previous life with it. Done
carelessly you get a device still signed into a parent's account, with a
parent's photos, a parent's saved passwords and a browser full of a parent's
history. Done properly it takes an evening.

## Wipe it first, always

Adding a child's account to a phone that still has yours on it is the mistake
that causes the most trouble later, because the two accounts share the device
and the supervision never quite behaves.

Google's own guidance before a factory reset is specific:

Know the Google account username and password currently on the phone. Confirm
the password works by signing in somewhere else first. If you changed that
password recently, **wait 24 hours before resetting** — this is the step people
skip and then find themselves locked out of a phone they own, because the
protection that stops a stolen handset being wiped and resold cannot tell the
difference between a thief and you.

Back up anything you want to keep. A factory reset erases the phone, and all
apps and their data are uninstalled with it.

Google asks for at least 70% battery and a wifi or mobile connection, because
the phone needs to be online to sign in once it restarts.

The reset itself lives in the Settings app on most phones, though the exact path
moves around between manufacturers — Google points people to their device
maker's support site for that reason rather than publishing one path that would
be wrong for half of Android.

## Then set it up as theirs

### Sign in with a child account, not yours

Create the account through Family Link if they do not have one. What you are
avoiding is a phone that technically belongs to you with a child using it, which
is a worse arrangement than it sounds — it makes every rule ambiguous.

### Add supervision before you hand it over

Install Family Link, set the daily limit, set bedtime, and set per-app limits.
Doing this before the handover matters more than the settings you choose,
because a phone that arrives with rules feels different from a phone that has
rules imposed on it a fortnight later.

### Set a Play Store PIN

In the Play app, tap the profile icon, then **Settings → Family → Content
restrictions → Go to parental controls → Set up a parental controls PIN**. Use a
PIN they have not seen you type. Without it the filters can be switched off by
the person they exist for.

### Check what survived

Open the app drawer and look at what is actually installed. A wiped phone
restores apps from whichever account signs in, so a child account that has
existed on a family tablet may pull down a surprising amount.

## Two things worth deciding before you hand it over

**Where it sleeps.** Decide this once, in advance, and preferably somewhere that
is not the bedroom. It prevents more trouble than any filter, and introducing it
later is almost impossible without it reading as a punishment.

**What unlocks next.** An old phone can do everything, so the restrictions are
entirely a matter of what you switch on. Say out loud what comes next and what
has to happen first. A phone with a direction is a different object from a phone
with a fence around it.

## Why the old phone is often the better option

A dedicated child's handset solves the first-phone problem by removing the
capability. That works well at nine and becomes the reason for another purchase
at thirteen, because the restrictions that made it right are the ones the child
grows into resenting.

Hardware you already own does not have that shape. The same device can relax as
the child does, which means the upgrade conversation is about permissions rather
than about buying something. It is also free, and the money is better spent on
the bike they are saving for.

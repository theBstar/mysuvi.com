Most households arrive at the same first solution. Pick a number of minutes,
enforce it with whatever the phone provides, and expect the argument to end.

It usually does end, for about two weeks. Then it returns in a different shape:
negotiation at the boundary, stopwatch lawyering, a child who has learned
exactly how long the parental controls take to kick in.

The failure is not willpower on either side. It is that a cap measures the one
thing that matters least.

## A cap has no opinion about content

Ninety minutes of maths practice and ninety minutes of autoplay video are
identical to a timer. Both decrement the same counter. A child who spends the
allowance on something demanding gets no credit for it, and a child who spends
it on whatever the algorithm serves is not penalised.

So the incentive a cap creates is not moderation. It is urgency. When time is
scarce and undifferentiated, the rational move is to spend it on whatever pays
off fastest, which is precisely the content parents worry about.

Pew's October 2025 survey of 3,054 parents of under-13s found 51% reporting
daily screen use, up from 43% in 2020, with much of the rise coming from
YouTube. Caps have been widespread across that whole period. Whatever they are
doing, they are not holding the line on duration.

## The timer becomes the relationship

The second problem is what enforcement does to the household.

Once a device is the authority, every conversation about it becomes a
conversation about the rule rather than the thing. Parents end up defending a
number they picked semi-arbitrarily, and children argue against the number
instead of explaining what they actually wanted the time for. Pew's 2024 work
on teens and parents found both sides describing screen time as a recurring
source of friction, which is not a surprise to anyone living it.

The number was supposed to remove the argument. It relocates it.

## What tends to work better

Three shifts, in rough order of how much they change:

- **Budget the floor, earn the rest.** Keep a baseline that needs no
  justification. Above that, time or features are earned through something
  concrete — reading, exercise, chores, practice. The child now has a lever, and
  the conversation moves from "how long" to "what for".
- **Reward with something that isn't screen time.** Paying for good habits in
  more screen time teaches that screens are the currency of value. Points toward
  a real thing — bike gear, a book, a day out — makes the same mechanic without
  that lesson. Parents we've spoken to are consistently clearer about this than
  the product literature is.
- **Make the rules visible to the child.** A rule a child can read, and ask you
  to change, is a rule they can practise judgment against. A rule that just
  happens to them is one they will only ever try to beat.

None of this removes the need for a limit. Bedtime is a limit and it should
stay one.

## The part that takes longest

A cap is fast to set and produces immediate compliance, which is why everyone
starts there. Earned freedom is slower and requires the parent to keep showing
up for the negotiation.

The payoff is that it has an exit. A child on a cap at ten is on a cap at
fifteen, and at eighteen has had no practice deciding anything. A child who has
spent years earning steps has been rehearsing the actual skill: working out
what a screen is worth to them this afternoon.

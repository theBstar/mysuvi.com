The phone usually arrives before the rules do. It is a birthday, or a new school
route, and the box gets opened in a good mood with a vague understanding that
there will be limits at some point.

The limits then get written during the first argument, which is the worst
available moment. Whatever is decided then reads as punishment, because it
arrived as one.

Doing it in the other order costs an evening and changes the next two years.

## Six things to settle first

**What is this phone for?** Name the actual job. Walking home, a bus route, a
parent working late. If the real answer is that everyone else has one, say that
out loud too — it is a legitimate reason, and naming it honestly stops it
masquerading as a safety argument later.

**What can it do on day one?** Be concrete and start narrower than feels
generous. Calls, messages to a known list, camera, music. Adding to that later
is easy and reads to a child as progress, whereas taking something away is a
fight every time.

**What unlocks next, and how?** This is the one most families skip, and it does
the most work. If a child knows a browser arrives after a specific demonstrated
thing, the phone has a direction. Without it, every request is a fresh
negotiation with no precedent.

**Where does it sleep?** Decide once, in advance, and preferably not in the
bedroom. This single rule prevents more trouble than any filter, and it is
almost impossible to introduce later without it reading as a sanction.

**What happens when something goes wrong?** Say explicitly that they can come to
you and keep the phone. A child who believes disclosure costs them the device
will try to handle things alone. That is the single most consequential sentence
in the whole conversation.

**When do we look at this again?** Put a date on it. A rule with a review date
is a rule a child can live with, because it is not permanent by default.

## Write it down

Not a contract with signatures. A note in a shared album, or a page stuck
somewhere, listing what was agreed.

The value is not enforcement. It is that six weeks later, when memories diverge,
there is a record neither of you is inventing on the spot. It also means the
child can point at it, which matters more than parents expect — being able to
cite the rule is part of feeling the rule is fair.

## What this does not fix

Pew's October 2025 survey found 51% of parents of under-13s reporting daily
screen use, up from 43% in 2020. No opening conversation reverses a trend like
that, and any article promising otherwise is selling something.

What it changes is the ground the next two years are argued on. Rules agreed in
advance get treated as an agreement. Rules imposed after a row get treated as a
loss, and children litigate losses.

That difference compounds.
